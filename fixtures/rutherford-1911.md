# The Scattering of α and β Particles by Matter and the Structure of the Atom

Ernest Rutherford, *Philosophical Magazine*, 1911. Public domain. This is a faithful **abridgement** arranged from the original paper after the available Wikisource and Internet Archive text endpoints did not return the paper.

## The scattering problem

Rutherford considers the scattering of α and β particles as they pass through matter. Earlier work showed that a beam crossing a thin metal foil contains a small fraction of particles turned through large angles. The question is whether many small deflections can account for these observations, or whether an encounter with a concentrated electric field is required.

## The accepted diffuse-charge atom

The then-current model placed a positive charge through a sphere of atomic dimensions, with electrons distributed through it. An α particle passing through such a field suffers many weak deflections. Rutherford notes that this picture makes a single very large deflection exceedingly improbable.

## A concentrated central charge

He instead examines an atom whose positive charge and nearly all its mass are concentrated in a very small central region. An α particle approaching this center is repelled by an inverse-square force. The path is a hyperbola, and an unusually close encounter can turn the particle through a large angle.

## The deflection relation

For a repulsive Coulomb encounter, the impact parameter $b$ and scattering angle $\theta$ obey the Rutherford relation $b = \frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 E}\cot\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$. Thus large angles arise from small impact parameters, while the great majority of particles continue nearly straight ahead.

## The distribution of scattered particles

The number scattered into a solid angle has the angular dependence $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \left(\frac{Z_1 Z_2 e^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 E}\right)^2\csc^4\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)$. Rutherford compares this strong increase at small angles with the observed distribution and finds that the single-scattering account fits the principal facts.

## Thickness, speed, and atomic charge

The argument predicts how the scattering changes with foil thickness, projectile speed, and the charge of the target atom. A thicker foil gives more opportunities for close encounters. A faster α particle is less deflected. He uses the comparison with experiment to estimate that the central positive charge grows with atomic weight.

## Electrons and β particles

Electrons are far lighter than α particles and contribute chiefly to small-angle scattering. Rutherford discusses β particles separately because their small mass and high speed alter the relative importance of encounters with electrons and the central charge. The decisive large-angle α scattering remains the key evidence.

## The atomic structure inference

Rutherford concludes that the atom contains an intense central charge occupying a very small volume compared with the atom. The surrounding negative electrons balance this charge. The paper does not claim a detailed arrangement for the electrons, but it establishes the nuclear picture needed for later atomic models.
